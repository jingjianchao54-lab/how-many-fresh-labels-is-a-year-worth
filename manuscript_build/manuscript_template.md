**Author:** «AUTHOR_BLOCK»

# Abstract

Relabelling decisions need the cost of old labels in labels, not in lost F1. We define the break-even size *n*^\*^(*g*), the number of current labels per class that matches a classifier trained on *N* labels per class collected *g* years earlier, and the label-equivalence ratio *ρ*(*g*) = *n*^\*^(*g*)/*N*. Under a pre-specified analysis plan, we estimated both on two ten-class community classification tasks, Reddit (2012–2018, *N* = «rd_N») and Stack Exchange (2015–2021, *N* = «se_N»), with author-disjoint splits and five replicates. For term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) features with logistic regression, six-year-old labels cost «rd_M1_C6» and «se_M1_C6» Macro-F1 points and were worth «rd_M1_rho6» and «se_M1_rho6» of as many current labels (one pair of years each); the pre-specified hypothesis of a ratio below one half was not supported. Under log-linear interpolation the ratio equals exp(−*C*/*s*), where *C* is the F1 cost and *s* the local learning-curve slope, so flat curves turn small F1 costs into large label costs. In added analyses, «rd_N» six-year-old Reddit labels per class matched about «rd_M6_n6» current ones for a fine-tuned Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) model and «rd_M5F_n6» for a frozen MiniLM encoder whose pre-training data overlap the study period.

**Keywords:** temporal drift; dataset shift; text classification; label efficiency; learning curves; social media; concept drift; reproducibility

# 1. Introduction

Classifiers that route, filter or tag user-generated text are trained on labelled examples that were collected at some point in the past and then used for months or years. Language on online platforms changes over that period: vocabulary and topics shift and words change their associations [@jaidka2018; @lukes2018; @huang2018]. Classifiers trained on older data tend to do worse on current data. The decline has been measured for sentiment, hate speech, topic classification and named-entity tasks, for sparse lexical models and for fine-tuned language models [@florio2020; @rottger2021; @luu2022; @agarwal2022; @alkhalifa2023; @ushio2024].

Almost all of this work reports the decline as a difference in a performance score, for example a loss of so many F1 points per year of misalignment [@luu2022]. For the person who maintains a deployed classifier, the pressing question is practical: should the existing labelled set be kept, topped up or replaced, and how many new labels would it take? A drop of one F1 point says little about that decision. Whether one point is cheap or expensive to win back depends on how quickly performance rises with more training data, that is, on the learning curve of the model on the task [@figueroa2012; @viering2023]. Two classifiers with equal F1 losses can be far apart once the loss is counted in labels.

This paper measures the effect of label age in the unit in which relabelling decisions are made. We ask how many labels per class from the test year are needed to match a classifier trained on *N* labels per class from *g* years earlier. We call this number the break-even size *n*^\*^(*g*) and the ratio *ρ*(*g*) = *n*^\*^(*g*)/*N* the label-equivalence ratio. A ratio of 0.9 means that an old label set is worth 90% of a current one of the same size; a ratio of 0.1 means that it is worth little more than a tenth.

Expressing an effect as an equivalent number of training examples is not new: it has been used to value prompts [@lescao2021] and pre-training [@hernandez2021]. The trade-off between recent and plentiful data is not new either. Methods for learning under concept drift choose how far back a training window should reach [@klinkenberg2000; @gama2014], theoretical work bounds the error of learning from a drifting stream or from a mixture of source and target samples [@bendavid2010; @mazzetto2023], and empirical studies of temporal drift have trained on old and new data together [@florio2020]. Empirical studies of temporal drift, however, either fix the amount of training data, as in the standard train-on-one-period design [@huang2018; @luu2022], or vary it without converting the result into an equivalent amount of current data. To our knowledge, no study has estimated, with uncertainty intervals, how many current labels an older label set is worth.

We estimated this quantity on two public, timestamped corpora in which the label is the community a text was posted in: ten Reddit communities from 2012 to 2018 and ten Stack Exchange sites from 2015 to 2021. Because the classes are communities whose definition does not change over time (the distinction between time-dependent and time-independent labels is drawn by Agarwal and Nenkova [@agarwal2022]), losses reflect change in the text and in the make-up of the corpora rather than a redefinition of the labels. This is a favourable setting for old labels, and the results should be read as such. Five lightweight classifiers were compared, from term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) features with logistic regression to a frozen MiniLM sentence encoder [@reimers2019; @wang2020minilm]. After the main results were known, a graphics processing unit (GPU) became available and two models were added: the MiniLM encoder with the full planned design and a fine-tuned Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) model [@devlin2019]. They are reported as an added analysis. The data gate, sampling scheme, models, hypotheses and tests were fixed in an analysis plan before any per-year count or score was computed, and every later change is reported.

The contributions are as follows.

1. We define the break-even size and the label-equivalence ratio for temporal drift, with an estimator based on log-linear interpolation along the current-year learning curve, a stratified paired bootstrap interval and a rule for censored estimates. We show that under this interpolation the ratio is exp(−*C*/*s*), where *C* is the F1 cost and *s* the slope of the learning curve per unit of log training size, so the ratio can be predicted from two quantities that are often already measured. The identity follows from the interpolation; what the study adds is the estimand, its estimator and intervals, and estimates obtained under a fixed plan.
2. We report estimates from a pre-specified analysis on two platforms and five lightweight models, with two transformer-based models added afterwards. The F1 cost of label age was small, at most «rng_C1_max» Macro-F1 points after one year and between «rng_C6_min» and «rng_C6_max» points after six years across all seven model settings. In labels, one-year-old sets were worth «rng_rho1_min»–«rng_rho1_max» of current ones for the three sparse lexical models and six-year-old sets «rng_rho6_min»–«rng_rho6_max». The hypothesis that six-year-old labels are worth less than half of current ones was not supported for the primary model.
3. We show, descriptively, that on Reddit-10 the slope varied enough between models, from sparse lexical classifiers to frozen and fine-tuned transformers, to separate ratios that F1 costs did not separate (on Stack Exchange-10 the intervals overlap), and we report when combining old and new labels helps and when it stops helping.

After a review of related work (Section 2), Section 3 sets out the quantities, data, models and statistical procedure. Results follow in Section 4; Section 5 discusses what they mean and where they stop, and Section 6 concludes.

# 2. Related Work

## 2.1. Temporal Degradation of Text Classifiers

Early studies of temporal effects in text classification trained a model on one period and tested it on others. Huang and Paul [@huang2018] trained logistic regression on TF-IDF n-grams for six corpora with timestamps, downsampled the training data so that every period had the same size, and found that classifiers generally performed best on the period they were trained on. Lukeš and Søgaard [@lukes2018] showed that lexical features in product reviews change polarity over a few years and that sparse models relying on highly predictive features suffer most. Jaidka et al. [@jaidka2018] studied how language models used for social-media prediction tasks degrade over a few years. He et al. [@he2018] proposed neural classifiers that carry information between successive time steps.

With pre-trained language models, attention moved from whether classifiers decay to how they can be kept current. Fine-tuning on labelled data from the target period helped more than continued pre-training on unlabelled text from that period, both for comments on social media [@rottger2021] and across eight tasks spanning five years or more, for which Luu et al. [@luu2022] defined a temporal degradation (TD) score as the slope of performance loss per year of misalignment; diachronic language models trained on successive periods of Twitter data were found to cope better with later tweets when updated continually [@loureiro2022], weights fine-tuned on different periods can be interpolated to serve intervening and later periods [@nylund2024], and pre-trained models can be aligned to a target year by prompting or fine-tuning [@zhao2024clock]. A second line of work asks which tasks decay. Agarwal and Nenkova [@agarwal2022] separated the deterioration of a fixed model from the benefit of training on recent data and found that deterioration is not always a concern when labels do not depend on time, whereas training on recent data helped in every case. Over datasets spanning 6 to 19 years, losses were weaker for closed-domain data such as book reviews than for open-domain social media [@alkhalifa2023], and on five Twitter tasks out-of-time training lost consistently against in-time training of the same size for entity- and event-driven tasks but not for topic or sentiment classification [@ushio2024]. Evaluation practice has moved in the same direction: benchmarks of distribution shift over time include text classification [@yao2022], language models degrade on text written after their training period [@lazaridou2021], random splits overestimate performance on new data [@sogaard2021], and measures of dataset drift have been proposed to predict performance on new data [@chang2023drift]. In all of this work the effect of time is expressed as a change in a score such as accuracy, F1 or perplexity.

## 2.2. Old and New Data: Windows, Weighting and Mixing

Work on concept drift treats the age of training data as a design variable. Klinkenberg and Joachims [@klinkenberg2000] chose the size of a sliding training window for support vector machines by minimising an estimate of the error, and surveys review strategies for detecting and adapting to drift [@gama2014], including detectors that monitor the performance of a deployed model [@bayram2022], methods for text streams [@garcia2025] and with attention to explainability [@pelosi2025]. Recent work in this journal has adapted detectors incrementally to drift in streaming data [@li2025addaeil]. On the theoretical side, Ben-David et al. [@bendavid2010] bounded the target error of a classifier trained on a weighted combination of source and target samples and showed that, under their bound, source data stop being useful once the target sample exceeds a threshold that depends on the complexity of the hypothesis class and the divergence between domains; Mazzetto and Upfal [@mazzetto2023] gave an algorithm that chooses how much past data to use under drift of unknown size. Closest to our mixing experiment, Florio et al. [@florio2020] added an older and larger hate-speech corpus to recent Italian training data and found that it helped a support vector machine but hurt a fine-tuned transformer.

## 2.3. Learning Curves and Equivalent Data

A learning curve describes how performance grows with training set size and is used to predict the value of additional data [@viering2023]. Figueroa et al. [@figueroa2012] fitted inverse power laws to small learning curves in order to estimate how many annotated samples a target accuracy requires, and Mahmood et al. [@mahmood2022] treated the amount of data to collect as an optimisation over collection cost and a performance target, which reduced the risk of missing the target compared with estimates from fitted scaling laws. Le Scao and Rush [@lescao2021] expressed the benefit of a prompt as the number of additional labelled examples it is worth, and Hernandez et al. [@hernandez2021] defined the data effectively transferred by pre-training as the amount of task data a model trained from scratch would need to reach the same loss. Our break-even size applies the same idea to label age: it locates the point on the current-year learning curve that an older training set reaches.

## 2.4. Position of This Study

Lightweight classifiers remain common in applied text classification, including on social-media text [@palanivinayagam2023; @padhy2024; @chahdi2025]. For such models, empirical studies of temporal drift either hold the training size fixed [@huang2018; @luu2022; @ushio2024] or vary it without expressing the outcome in labels [@florio2020], and the theory [@bendavid2010] depends on quantities that are hard to estimate for a given task. This study measures directly, with intervals, how many current labels an older label set is worth, how this exchange rate changes with the age of the labels and with the model, and how it follows from the F1 cost and the learning-curve slope.

# 3. Materials and Methods

## 3.1. Quantities and Hypotheses

Let *S*(*t* → *u*) be the Macro-F1 on the test set of year *u* of a classifier trained on *N* labels per class from year *t*, and let *F*(*n*, *u*) be the Macro-F1 on the same test set of a classifier trained on *n* labels per class from year *u*, so that *S*(*u* → *u*) = *F*(*N*, *u*). Macro-F1 is reported on the 0–1 scale; costs and differences between scores are reported in Macro-F1 points, where one point is 0.01. For a gap of *g* ≥ 1 years, let *U*~*g*~ be the test years *u* for which year *u* − *g* lies in the study window. The staleness cost is

$$C(g) = \frac{1}{|U_g|} \sum_{u \in U_g} \left( S(u \to u) - S(u-g \to u) \right) \qquad (1)$$

The backward cost, with the training year after the test year, is defined in the same way and reported separately. To estimate the break-even size, the current-year learning curve $\overline{F}_g(n)$ and the stale score $\overline{S}_g$ are averaged over the test years in *U*~*g*~ and over replicates, so that $C(g) = \overline{F}_g(N) - \overline{S}_g$. The break-even size *n*^\*^(*g*) is the smallest *n* at which $\overline{F}_g(n)$ reaches $\overline{S}_g$, found by linear interpolation in log *n* between the grid sizes. Because it is the first crossing, it is defined even if the curve is not monotone. If $\overline{F}_g(N) < \overline{S}_g$, the estimate is censored and reported as "> *N*"; if the smallest grid size already reaches $\overline{S}_g$, it is reported as "< 25". The label-equivalence ratio is *ρ*(*g*) = *n*^\*^(*g*)/*N*.

The ratio is tied to the F1 cost by the slope of the learning curve. If *n*^\*^(*g*) lies in the top grid segment [*n*~*k*~, *N*], the interpolated curve there is $\overline{F}_g(n) = \overline{F}_g(N) - s_g \ln(N/n)$ with slope $s_g = [\overline{F}_g(N) - \overline{F}_g(n_k)]/\ln(N/n_k)$, and setting $\overline{F}_g(n^{\ast}) = \overline{S}_g$ gives

$$\rho(g) = \exp\left( -\frac{C(g)}{s_g} \right) \qquad (2)$$

More generally, if *n*^\*^(*g*) lies in the segment [*n*~*j*~, *n*~*j*+1~] with slope *s*~*g*,*j*~, the same argument gives $\rho(g) = (n_{j+1}/N)\exp\left(-[\overline{F}_g(n_{j+1}) - \overline{S}_g]/s_{g,j}\right)$, of which Equation (2) is the case *n*~*j*+1~ = *N*. Equation (2) requires *s*~*g*~ > 0 and *C*(*g*) ≥ 0; a negative cost makes the estimate censored above *N*. That held throughout: every averaged current-year learning curve in this study rose between each pair of adjacent grid sizes. Lower down the curve the same expression, with the top-segment slope, is only an approximation, and the estimates in Table 4 always come from the interpolation itself. So an F1 cost is cheap in labels where the curve is steep, and dear where it is flat. The identity is a property of the estimator rather than a further estimate; Section 5 uses it to explain why the models differ.

Three hypotheses were specified for the primary model on each dataset: H1, the cost after one year is positive (the lower 95% bound of *C*(1) is above zero); H2, the cost grows with the gap (the 95% interval of *C*(*g*~max~) − *C*(1) is above zero); and H3, labels lose more than half their value by the largest gap (*ρ*(*g*~max~) < 0.5, supported if the upper 95% bound of *n*^\*^(*g*~max~) is below *N*/2, not supported if the lower bound is at or above *N*/2, and inconclusive otherwise). A staleness cost below 0.010 Macro-F1 (1 point) was declared in advance to be described as practically small whatever its interval.

## 3.2. Data and Data Gate

Two public corpora were used (Table 1). The unit of analysis is a single post, and its label is the community in which it was posted.

*Reddit-10.* Ten subreddit corpora distributed with ConvoKit [@chang2020convokit] (chess, Coffee, Cooking, gardening, history, investing, languagelearning, philosophy, running and woodworking). Each utterance (post or comment) is labelled with its subreddit and dated by the calendar year of its timestamp in Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). The corpora end on 31 October 2018, so the last year is incomplete. Utterances were excluded if the text was missing, empty, deleted or removed, if the author was deleted, if the author name matched a bot pattern, or if it repeated a text the same author had already posted in the same subreddit (the first occurrence in the file was kept). Tokens equal to a subreddit name were removed so that a post could not be classified by naming its community. Because several names are also ordinary words (for example "chess", "cooking" or "running"), this removes some content words as well; the unmasked text is a sensitivity analysis.

*Stack Exchange-10.* Questions, answers and comments from ten Stack Exchange sites (philosophy, photo, music, history, cooking, movies, bicycles, boardgames, gardening and chess) in the public data dump of April 2024 hosted by the Internet Archive. The label is the site and the year is that of the creation date. Markup was removed, and items were excluded if the text, date or user was missing, if the author was the community account, or if they repeated a text the same user had already posted on the same site. The three post types were pooled. The year 2024 was excluded because the dump ends early in that year. Site names were not masked.

For both datasets, text was lower-cased, web addresses were removed, tokens were matched with the pattern `[a-z0-9]+(?:'[a-z]+)?`, items with fewer than 10 tokens were dropped and texts were truncated to 256 tokens.

A cell is a (class, year) pair. A cell is eligible at size (*N*, *V*, *T*) if it contains at least 2*N* training-pool items, *V* validation items and *T* test items after the split described below. The plan fixed a ladder of three sizes and required all ten classes to be eligible in at least six consecutive years; the largest size meeting this rule and the longest run of years at that size were taken. Reddit-10 passed at the largest size and Stack Exchange-10 at the smallest (Table 1).

The second dataset originally registered was the HuffPost News Category corpus. It failed the gate at every size, because only two categories had enough items in five consecutive years. As the plan required, work on it stopped, and Stack Exchange was registered as its replacement before any Stack Exchange file was downloaded or counted.

## 3.3. Splits and Sampling

Authors were assigned to training, validation and test sets by a hash: *h* = SHA-256("paper5|" + author key), where SHA-256 is the 256-bit Secure Hash Algorithm, with the first 8 bytes read as an integer and divided by 2^64^; authors with *h* < 0.60 went to training, 0.60 ≤ *h* < 0.70 to validation and *h* ≥ 0.70 to test. The author key is the user name on Reddit and the site-specific user identifier on Stack Exchange. The assignment is global across years and, on Reddit, across classes, so no Reddit account and no Stack Exchange site account contributes to both the training and the evaluation side of any comparison; a person holding accounts on two Stack Exchange sites may appear on both sides. To limit the influence of prolific authors, at most five items per author were kept in each cell (the earliest five). From each cell, a test set of *T* items, a validation set of *V* items and a training pool of 2*N* items were drawn once. Each of five replicates drew *N* training items per cell from the pool with a random generator seeded by (replicate, class, year); smaller training sizes are prefixes of that draw, so the learning curve is nested within each replicate. Every class contributes the same number of training and test items, so training and test sets are balanced.

## 3.4. Models and Hyper-Parameter Selection

Five classifiers were compared (Table 2), all implemented with scikit-learn [@pedregosa2011] or NumPy. M1, the primary model, uses TF-IDF features of word unigrams and bigrams (minimum document frequency 2, sublinear term frequency, at most 200,000 features, fitted on the training set only) with multinomial logistic regression. M2 replaces logistic regression with a linear support vector machine (SVM). M3 is complement naive Bayes on unigram and bigram counts [@rennie2003]. M4 is logistic regression on the mean of 100-dimensional Global Vectors (GloVe) [@pennington2014] of in-vocabulary tokens. M5 is logistic regression on mean-pooled, L2-normalised vectors from the frozen all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence encoder [@reimers2019; @wang2020minilm]. M5 receives the original text with case preserved, web addresses removed and, on Reddit, subreddit names removed, truncated to 128 word pieces. It was run with a NumPy implementation of the encoder (weights file SHA-256 53aa5117…, full hash in the repository) whose cosine similarities on the three reference sentences published with the model matched the published values to four decimal places.

Two models were added under amendment A-03 (Table 3, D8). M5F is M5 with the design the plan specified: every sampled item encoded with up to 256 word pieces, five replicates and all three experiments. It was run with the Transformers implementation [@wolf2020], which reproduced the same reference similarities. M6 is bert-base-uncased [@devlin2019] fine-tuned end to end, with a linear classification layer on the representation of the classification ([CLS]) token. Its recipe was fixed before any M6 score was computed and applied unchanged to every training run: input truncated to 128 word pieces; AdamW [@loshchilov2019] with weight decay 0.01 and batch size 32; linear warm-up over the first 10% of steps, then linear decay; the larger of 3 and ⌈150/*b*⌉ epochs, capped at 30, where *b* is the number of batches per epoch, so that small training sets still receive at least 150 updates; the last epoch used, without early stopping. M6 received the same text as M5. Only the learning rate was selected. Because of its cost, E3 for M6 was run on replicate 1 only.

Hyper-parameters were chosen separately for each dataset, model and training size by mean validation Macro-F1 over training years, using validation data from the same year as the training data and replicate 1; ties went to the smaller value. The chosen value was then used for every year and replicate. The selected value can change along the learning curve (Table A1); for example, M1 on Reddit-10 used *C* = 1 up to 200 labels per class and *C* = 10 from 500 upwards. Selecting the value at each size makes each point of the learning curve the classifier that would be trained with that many current labels, which is the comparison the break-even size is meant to support; fixing the value chosen for *N* would value current labels with a model tuned for a different amount of data. M6 selected the larger of its two learning rates at every size on both datasets, so a wider grid might have chosen a larger one. No time weighting or temporal adaptation was applied, by design.

## 3.5. Experiments

*E1 (ageing grid).* Each model was trained on year *t* with *N* labels per class and scored on the test set of every year *u* in the window, giving *S*(*t* → *u*). The forward and backward costs and the TD score of Luu et al. [@luu2022] were computed from this grid.

*E2 (exchange rate).* For each test year *u*, each model was trained on *n* labels per class from year *u* for every grid size *n* ≤ *N*, giving *F*(*n*, *u*); *n*^\*^(*g*) and *ρ*(*g*) were then computed as in Section 3.1.

*E3 (old plus new).* For *g* ∈ {1, *g*~max~} and every grid size *n* < *N*, each model was trained on the *N* old labels of year *u* − *g* concatenated, without weights, with *n* labels of year *u*. The result was compared with the current-only model of size *n* and the old-only model of size *N*; the hyper-parameter value chosen for size *N* was used.

*E4 (vocabulary drift, descriptive).* For each class and pair of years, the Jensen–Shannon divergence [@lin1991] between the unigram distributions of the two training pools was computed and correlated (Spearman) with the per-class F1 loss.

*E5 (sensitivity).* The primary model was rerun with the old training set at half the cell size and, on Reddit-10, without masking community names.

## 3.6. Statistical Inference

Intervals are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals from 2,000 resamples of test items within each (class, year) test cell (random seed 20261009). The same resamples were used for every model and condition, so comparisons are paired; replicate scores were averaged within each resample. Intervals for *n*^\*^(*g*) repeat the interpolation in each resample and keep censored resamples as censored when percentiles are taken. One-sided bootstrap *p*-values for H1–H3 were adjusted with the Holm procedure [@holm1979] within each dataset. A *p*-value is the share of resamples at or below the null value; where no resample was, *p* < 0.0005 before adjustment. These intervals describe test-set sampling for the training draws used. They do not include variation between training draws, which we report separately as the standard deviation across the five replicates, nor variation between calendar years, and they treat test items as independent although an author can contribute up to five items to a test cell. They also do not include uncertainty from the choice of interpolation between grid sizes.

## 3.7. Pre-Specification, Locking and Deviations

The analysis plan was frozen, with its SHA-256 hash recorded, before any per-year count, split size or score was computed. It was not deposited in a public registry; it is released with the code, together with its hash and the time-stamped amendment log. The Reddit corpora had been used in the author's earlier work on user-level prediction, so their overall size and time range were known, and a user-level classifier over whole posting histories had been trained in that work; no post-level classifier and no cross-year score had been computed on them before the plan was fixed. The data gate was applied to counts only. Hyper-parameters were then selected on validation data, and the selected values, the plan hash, the sample index files and the hashes of the evaluation code were written to a lock file before any test set was scored. Test predictions were produced once. Scoring was repeated only to add the MiniLM results, to run the sensitivity analyses, to apply the printing correction D7 and to add M5F and M6, always with the same bootstrap seed. Point estimates for Figure 3, Table A2 and the replicate checks in Section 4.3 were computed later from the stored predictions, without refitting (amendment log X-06, X-11, X-12). The two added models were entered in the amendment log before any of their scores existed and were selected and locked in their own lock files before their test predictions were made. Their scores were written to new result files, and a key-by-key comparison confirmed that the results of M1 were unchanged. Table 3 lists every deviation from the plan.

## 3.8. Implementation

M1–M5 ran on a two-core central processing unit (CPU) without a GPU, in Python 3.13.16 with NumPy 2.5.3, SciPy 1.18.1 and scikit-learn 1.9.1. M5F and M6 ran on a server with four NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPUs, in Python 3.10.12 with PyTorch 2.14.1 and Transformers 5.19.0, using bfloat16 mixed precision; both environments are recorded in the repository (`results/env.json` and `results/env_gpu.json`), and the bert-base-uncased weights were taken from a mirror of the public model repository and matched the published file hash. Code, analysis plan, amendment log, lock files, sample indices, per-run predictions, result files and the scripts that produce every table and figure are available in the repository listed in the Data Availability Statement. Every reported number is produced by the released scripts from the stored predictions. Figure 3 and the slope values in Section 5 use the learning curves averaged over *U*~*g*~, which the scoring script computes internally but did not store; they were recomputed from the stored test predictions without refitting and reproduce the reported break-even sizes to within 1.0 label per class.

# 4. Results

## 4.1. Same-Year Performance and the Ageing Grid

Trained and tested within the same year at the full cell size, the models reached Macro-F1 between «rd_M4_F1» and «rd_M6_F1» on Reddit-10 and between «se_M4_F1» and «se_M6_F1» on Stack Exchange-10 (Table 4). Fine-tuned BERT scored highest on both datasets, followed by the two MiniLM variants, and the GloVe average lowest. Replicate-to-replicate standard deviations of the cell scores were at most «rd_M1_sdmax» F1 points on Reddit-10 and «se_M1_sdmax» points on Stack Exchange-10 for the primary model.

## 4.2. Staleness Cost

For the primary model, one-year-old labels cost «rd_M1_C1» Macro-F1 points «rd_M1_C1ci» on Reddit-10 and «se_M1_C1» «se_M1_C1ci» on Stack Exchange-10. Six-year-old labels cost «rd_M1_C6» «rd_M1_C6ci» and «se_M1_C6» «se_M1_C6ci» points, and the increase from one to six years was «rd_M1_D61» «rd_M1_D61ci» and «se_M1_D61» «se_M1_D61ci» points. H1 and H2 were supported on both datasets (largest Holm-adjusted bootstrap *p* = 0.0015). By the pre-specified threshold, the one-year cost is practically small. When only *n* current labels per class are available, adding the *N* old labels helped every sparse model by a large margin at small *n*, and adding the *n* current labels to the old set helped more as *n* grew (Figure 5, Table 5). # 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Small F1 Costs, Large Label Costs

The F1 cost of label age in these tasks is small. Even after six years no model lost more than «rng_C6_max» Macro-F1 points, and after one year none lost more than «rng_C1_max». Read in isolation, these numbers suggest that relabelling can wait. The label-equivalence ratio gives a different picture: for the primary model on Reddit-10, a six-year-old set of «rd_N» labels per class was worth «rd_M1_n6» current labels, roughly a third less.

Why do the two views differ? Because the ratio is exp(−*C*/*s*) (Equation (2)), a fixed F1 cost *C* becomes a large loss of labels when the slope *s* of the learning curve is small. On Reddit-10, on the test year 2018 (the only one with a six-year gap), going from «rd_size_below_top» to «rd_N» current labels per class added «rd_M1_g6_topgain» F1 points for the primary model, «rd_M6_g6_topgain» points for fine-tuned BERT, «rd_M4_g6_topgain» points for GloVe and «rd_M5F_g6_topgain» points for MiniLM. The slopes per unit of log training size were «rd_M1_g6_slope», «rd_M6_g6_slope», «rd_M4_g6_slope» and «rd_M5F_g6_slope» points, and exp(−*C*/*s*) with these slopes gives «rd_M1_g6_rhoapprox», «rd_M6_g6_rhoapprox», «rd_M4_g6_rhoapprox» and «rd_M5F_g6_rhoapprox», against estimated ratios of «rd_M1_rho6», «rd_M6_rho6», «rd_M4_rho6» and «rd_M5F_rho6». The agreement is exact for the primary model, whose break-even size lies in the top segment. BERT and GloVe both have break-even sizes one segment lower; the approximation is close for BERT, whose curve has a similar slope in the two segments, and poorer for GloVe, whose curve is much steeper below the top segment. MiniLM lies two segments lower, and the approximation is rough. GloVe and MiniLM lost less F1 on Reddit-10 than the primary model, yet both lost a larger share of the value of their labels, because their curves were flatter. Fine-tuned BERT shows that this is not a property of weak models only: it had the highest same-year F1 and an F1 cost close to that of the primary model, but its curve was flatter at the top, and six-year-old labels kept about a third of their value rather than two thirds. Nor do low ratios require pre-training on the platforms studied. GloVe was trained on Wikipedia and Gigaword news text [@pennington2014] and BERT on books and Wikipedia [@devlin2019], neither of which includes Reddit or Stack Exchange, yet on Reddit-10 both kept only about a third of the value of six-year-old labels. The overlap between the training data of MiniLM and the study period (Section 5.4) may contribute to its still lower ratio; our design cannot separate the two. A model that plateaus early makes old labels cheap to replace; for the same reason, an old label set adds little to it. These comparisons are descriptive: the transformer-based models were added after the main results, and MiniLM was pre-trained on text from the study period (Section 5.4).

The half-size analysis fits this account: with fewer old labels, the current-year curve is steeper at the size being valued, and the ratio was higher. Any reported ratio should come with the size *N* at which it was measured. The slope *s* is worth reporting too, since an F1 cost and a learning curve are enough to predict the ratio without new experiments.

## 5.2. Practical Reading

For sparse lexical classifiers of community membership, trained on a few hundred to a few thousand labels per class, a label set lost roughly a tenth to a fifth of its value in the first year and roughly a third to two fifths over six years. For M1–M4, old and new labels combined did at least as well as either set alone, by point estimate, in every setting we ran; nothing in these data argues for discarding an old set. The few negative point estimates were small and all involved the transformer-based models (Section 4.4, Table 5). MiniLM on Reddit-10 with six-year-old labels is the exception. About «rd_M5F_n6» current labels per class were worth as much as «rd_N» old ones, and adding the old set to many current labels brought no gain. For fine-tuned BERT on Reddit-10, a six-year-old set kept about a third of its value, yet adding it to the current labels still helped in every setting we ran, on one replicate. These figures belong to the tasks studied. Before they guide spending elsewhere they should be re-estimated on the target task, which needs only a timestamped labelled set and a learning curve per year.

## 5.3. Relation to Previous Findings

The direction of our results agrees with earlier work: performance declined with temporal distance [@huang2018; @alkhalifa2023], training on recent data helped [@agarwal2022; @luu2022], and older data helped a linear model when mixed with recent data [@florio2020]. Florio et al. found that adding older data hurt a fine-tuned transformer; we found no gain, but no clear harm, for a frozen encoder with six-year-old labels, and a gain for fine-tuned BERT. Their older corpus came from a different source and annotation effort, whereas our old and new labels share one definition, which may explain the difference; with one replicate for this comparison we do not press it. The costs we observed are small, in line with the small losses Ushio and Camacho-Collados reported for topic classification and in contrast to the larger losses for entity- and event-driven tasks [@ushio2024] and for hate speech [@florio2020]. Community membership is defined by topic and style, and the meaning of the labels does not change over time; this suggests, but does not show, that our estimates lie at the favourable end of temporal drift.

## 5.4. Limitations

Several limitations qualify these results. Stack Exchange-10 passed the data gate only at the smallest size (*N* = «se_N»). In both datasets the six-year gap rests on a single pair of years, so *ρ*(1) and *ρ*(6) are averaged over different test years; at shorter gaps, ratios of single pairs of years varied by up to «se_yp_M1_spread» for the primary model (Table A2).

The intervals are probably too narrow. They cover test-set sampling only: variation between training draws is reported as replicate standard deviations but not built into the intervals, variation between calendar years is not modelled, and items from the same author are treated as independent. The break-even size is interpolated on a coarse grid, and because the selected hyper-parameter can change between grid sizes, the learning curve may have kinks. On Stack Exchange-10 several upper bounds of *n*^\*^(*g*) were censored at *N* (Table 4), so ratios close to one are poorly determined there.

The two transformer-based models were added after the results of M1–M5 were known (Table 3, D8). Their design, grids and scoring were registered before any of their scores existed, but the decision to add them was not, so they are reported descriptively and the hypothesis tests refer to the primary model only. According to its model card [@minilmcard], the MiniLM encoder was trained on data that include Reddit comments from 2015 to 2018 and Stack Exchange question–answer pairs; these overlap the years studied, so its results are not a clean out-of-time test. BERT was pre-trained on books and English Wikipedia [@devlin2019], which contain neither platform. For M6 the recipe was fixed, the grid held two learning rates (the larger was always selected) and E3 used one replicate; other fine-tuning settings could change the learning curve and with it the ratios. Table 4 keeps the earlier reduced MiniLM run (M5) for completeness. E3 pooled old and current labels without weights; a weighted combination of the two [@bendavid2010] might make better use of the current labels. No time-weighted training or adaptation method was tested, by design.

The tasks classify posts by community. Where label meaning drifts, costs and ratios may be very different. Within the data, the Reddit masking removes some content words, the last Reddit year is incomplete and the mix of Stack Exchange post types may shift over time. The analysis plan was not publicly registered, the registered second dataset failed the data gate and was replaced, and one printed interval bound was corrected after scoring (Table 3).

# 6. Conclusions

The cost of old labels can be stated in labels. A break-even size and a label-equivalence ratio do this, and both can be estimated with intervals from a timestamped labelled set. On two community classification tasks, label age cost little F1 but a substantial share of the value of a label set: for sparse lexical models, roughly a tenth to a fifth after one year and a third to two fifths after six years. The pre-specified hypothesis that six-year-old labels are worth less than half of current ones was not supported for the primary model, on the single pair of years available at that gap in each dataset. Under log-linear interpolation the ratio equals exp(−*C*/*s*), so models with flat learning curves turn small F1 costs into large label costs. On Reddit, six-year-old labels kept about two thirds of their value for the primary model, a third for a fine-tuned BERT model pre-trained without either platform and a tenth for a frozen sentence encoder whose pre-training data overlap the study period. We suggest that studies of temporal drift report learning curves alongside the usual train-on-one-period grid, so that their results can be read in labels as well as in F1.
